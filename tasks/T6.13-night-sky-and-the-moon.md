# T6.13 — At night the sky is not a sun, and the moon has a shape

Size: M · Prereqs: T6.12 · Files: `server/providers_openmeteo.py`, `server/server.py`,
`server/tests/`, `web/js/format.js`, `web/test/format.test.js`, `web/themes/neon/theme.js`,
`web/themes/neon/theme.css`, `e2e/layout/stress.js`, `docs/THEMING.md`

Requires: Firefox on `PATH` for the layout checks. Nothing here needs the phone.

**Asked for from the chair on 2026-09-21 at 21:20**, with the panel lit: the weather card was
drawing a **sun** for `Predominantemente limpo`, in the dark. Then: can the panel know which
moon to draw?

Both halves were checked before this file was written, not recalled:

- `GET api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast?...&current=temperature_2m,weather_code,is_day` answered
  `{"weather_code": 1, "is_day": 0}` for São Paulo at 21:15 that evening. The field exists, the
  server simply never asked for it.
- The moon's phase is arithmetic over the date and needs no network at all. **The first cut of
  this paragraph compared two different days** and is corrected here: the synodic model puts the
  moon at 10.16 days old and 78% lit at 2026-09-22T00:27Z, and the 9.85 days it was checked
  against is moongiant.com's page for the **21st**, not the 22nd. For the 22nd that site says
  10.77 days and 83%, and the USNO's own new moon — 2026-09-11 03:27 UTC — puts the true age at
  10.88 days. So the model runs about 0.6 of a day **young** there, not old, which is the
  opposite of what the numbers as first written implied.

  That error is the whole reason the buckets are wide. Checked since against the USNO's tables
  for 2026 and 2027 — 99 primary phases, `aa.usno.navy.mil/api/moon/phases/year` — the mean
  synodic model names **every one of them correctly**, with a worst error of 0.87 days against a
  bucket half-width of 1.85. It is a naming model, not an ephemeris, and the task's fixtures say
  so.

## Goal

A clear night draws stars rather than a sun, and **the moon is on the card all the time** — its
phase as a shape and how much of it is lit as a number, beside the temperature.

The second half grew during the work, from the chair: the moon started as a night-only glyph and
was asked for as permanent information. It is the slowest-moving fact the panel shows, about
three points of illumination a day, and the only one a reader can check against the sky.

**And the phase comes from an API rather than from arithmetic**, also asked for from the chair.
The task was first written around a local synodic model; the model survives as the fallback and
is now measurably the worse answer. For 2026-09-22T00:27Z:

| source | age | lit |
|---|---|---|
| USNO's own New Moon of 2026-09-11 03:27 UTC | 10.875 d | — |
| moongiant.com, consulted independently | 10.77 d | 83% |
| this task, deriving from the USNO's lunation | 10.88 d | 84% |
| the mean synodic fallback | 10.16 d | 78% |

## Steps

1. **`is_day` rides the weather object.** Add it to the `current=` list in
   `providers_openmeteo.py` and normalise it to a boolean `isDay` beside `tempC` and `code`.

   It goes **inside `weather`**, and so does the moon, which is the reason this is M and not L:
   `DataPayload.merge` copies that object whole and only strips its `stale`, so a key inside it
   reaches the page with no Java change. A key at the payload's top level would be the three-edit
   trap that cost T6.4 an evening.

   Absent means **day**, and so does an explicit `null` — the second half was the wave-18
   review's finding: `bool(current.get("is_day", 1))` defaults only the *missing* key, and
   open-meteo sends `null` for a current field it cannot compute. `bool(None)` is `False`, so
   the one value meaning *no answer* would have become the decisive answer for night.

2. **`server/providers_usno.py`** asks `aa.usno.navy.mil/api/moon/phases/date` for the primary
   phases around now and derives the phase from where now falls between two New Moons. No key,
   no registration; the USNO is what almanacs are checked against.

   The window has to *bracket* the moment, and the first cut of its two constants did not: six
   phases from 45 days back spans 44 days, so the window could end before today, `phase_at`
   correctly returned None, and every panel would have run on the fallback while looking exactly
   as if it were running on the API. Nine phases from 35 days back spans 66. There is a test
   whose only job is that.

   `moon_phase` never raises. It falls back to the mean synodic month and says
   `source: "mean"` out loud, so a payload is never ambiguous about which answered.

3. **`weatherGlyph(code, isDay)`** returns the night name for the two codes that are about the
   sky rather than about the weather — `clear` and `cloudy` — and is unchanged for the other
   six. Rain at night is still rain. The parameter is optional and defaults to day, so an older
   server behaves exactly as it did.

   `clear` at night is **stars, not a moon**: the moon has its own permanent place on the card
   now, and drawing it twice would be the card saying one thing in two sizes.

4. **The moon's shape is one filled path built from the lit fraction.** A circle with an outline
   is a circle; what says *moon* is the lit part being solid and the dark part not being drawn.
   Two arcs — the limb, and a terminator whose horizontal radius is `r * |1 - 2k|` — so the same
   `d` draws every phase, with the sweep flag deciding whether it bulges in (crescent) or out
   (gibbous).

   **The whole disc is drawn behind it as a thin ring.** That was the second cut and it was
   measured on the device: at 85% the lit shape alone is 16 units wide against a full 18, and at
   22px that reads as a plain oval saying nothing about phase. With the ring to compare against,
   85% reads as almost full. It also stops the new moon being a special case — a ring with
   nothing filled in is what a new moon is.

   Lit on the **left** when waxing: this panel is in São Paulo and the southern hemisphere sees
   the terminator on the opposite side from the northern one. One constant, documented rather
   than configured.

5. **The stress fixture gains a night, and `e2e/layout/night.js` is a fifth pass.** The stress
   payload carries the widest moon the card can be asked to draw — a three-digit percentage
   beside the widest range — and the night fixture draws a clear sky after dark at first
   quarter, the one phase whose terminator is a straight line and so the arc this geometry is
   most likely to get wrong.

```bash
python -c "import sys; sys.path.insert(0,'.'); from server.providers_openmeteo import normalise; sys.exit(0 if normalise({'current':{'is_day':None}})['isDay'] is True else 1)"
```

The last two lines are the ones that would otherwise rot. The first asserts the field survives
normalisation as a **boolean false** rather than as the `0` the upstream sends; the second that
an explicit `null` means day, which is the difference between a moon and a sun on a panel whose
page does `if (isDay)`.

## Manual check

On the device, after dark, with a clear sky: the card draws the moon, and the moon is the one
in the window. That last half is the only check that matters and no harness can make it — the
whole point of drawing a phase rather than a generic crescent is that somebody can look up.

## Notes

- **The night profile is not the night.** `isNight` (T6.4) answers a *config window* the owner
  chose for dimming the panel, typically 22:00–06:00. The sun sets at 18:00 in June here, and a
  glyph keyed to the dimming window would draw a sun for four hours of darkness — which is the
  bug this task is fixing, moved four hours later. `is_day` comes from the city's own
  coordinates and is the only thing here that knows where the sun is.
- **Eight buckets, not four.** The ask named minguante, crescente and cheia; waxing and waning
  crescents are mirror images and a panel that drew one for both would be wrong half the month
  in the one way a reader can check by looking out of the window.
- The southern hemisphere sees the terminator on the opposite side from the northern one. The
  panel is in São Paulo; drawing the northern convention there would be wrong every night, so
  the lit limb follows the latitude the server already geocodes if that is cheap, and is
  otherwise documented as a known simplification rather than left unsaid.
